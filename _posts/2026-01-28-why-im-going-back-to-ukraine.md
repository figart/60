title: "Why I'm going back to Ukraine"

The worst cappuccino I ever had was in Folkestone, England—a coastal town where, eighty years earlier, tens of thousands of soldiers walked down the very same street on their way to the Channel. They were headed to war. I was headed to a ferry, across to Dunkirk, through France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Germany and Poland, and finally into Ukraine—with a convoy of donated vehicles.

My 60th birthday arrived at midnight just after driving off the ferry, and my comrades celebrated the occasion with me only minutes later by singing me happy birthday at an Esso roadside fuel station and rest stop just up the coast from Dunkirk. After that the days blurred, driving twelve hours at a stretch trying to keep up with a British crew who have done this run literally dozens of times.

That was last March. I'd come across the opportunity thanks to a fellow Rotarian who invited me to go with him. It's largely a Rotary effort, with volunteers from various clubs throughout the UK, though as with most any successful effort there are a couple of linchpins that make it work. In the end, though, it's ordinary people buying used vehicles, patching them up, filling them with supplies, and driving them straight into Ukraine. No bureaucracy. No shipping containers. Just keys in hand, delivered to people who need them. On my first trip, many of the vehicles were being used to rescue the wounded and evacuate displaced civilians. There are still a lot of rescues happening, but more and more these vehicles are being used to sustain the war mission.

I almost didn't go. Not because I was afraid of what might happen in Ukraine—I've always had a bit of the adventurer in me—but because I'd come down with a nasty cough days before my flight and wasn't sure I'd recover in time. I spent $700 to delay my departure, then spent the next 48 hours wondering if I should just stay home. Thank goodness I didn't. But the cough came with me across the Channel, through the checkpoints, past the soldiers stationed at every small bridge, all the way to Lviv.

And now I'm going back.

---

I've been trying to articulate why.

It's not the adventure, though I won't pretend the adventure isn't part of it. Driving across Europe in a convoy feels like something—purposeful, kinetic, alive. But I've had plenty of adventures. This is different.

Part of it is the people. The Ukrainians I met weren't victims waiting to be saved. They were doctors running hospitals, widows building organizations to help other widows, comedians still telling jokes, printers still running presses. They didn't need my pity. They needed vehicles. So that's what we brought.

Part of it is the energy. There's no other word for it. At the border crossing back into Poland, after days inside Ukraine, I felt something I can only describe as _dense_—the accumulated weight of everyone who's crossed that line in the last three years. Refugees. Soldiers. Mothers with children. Aid workers. Journalists. Me, some guy from Texas who showed up with a donated Land Rover and not much else. The border holds all of it. You feel it in your chest.

And part of it—I'll just say it—is that a war zone felt less threatening than home.

I don't mean that literally. I wasn't dodging missiles. The front lines were hundreds of miles east. What I mean is this: In Ukraine, people know what they're fighting for. There's clarity. Purpose. A shared understanding that something precious is at stake and worth defending.

Back home, I'm not so sure we know what we're defending anymore. Or who we've become. I watch the news and feel something between grief and nausea. I'm not here to lecture anyone about politics—people believe what they believe, and I've got no interest in pretending I'm better than my neighbors. But I can say this much: something is terribly wrong. And being in Ukraine, surrounded by people who've had everything upended and are still building, still helping, still laughing—it made me feel less alone in that unease.

So I'm going back.

---

In five weeks, I'll fly to London, meet up with the convoy, and do it again. This time I'm raising $5,000 to help cover the cost of one vehicle—its purchase, repairs, and paperwork. Once again, I'll drive east across Europe, straight into Ukraine, and hand the keys to someone who needs it.

[Photo placeholder: The convoy crossing into Ukraine]

Every dollar goes toward the vehicle and the delivery. 100%. I cover my own travel.

> If you've ever wanted to do something direct—something where you can see exactly where your help goes—this is it. A car becomes a lifeline. An ambulance becomes a hospital on wheels. A Land Rover becomes the thing that gets supplies to a village that can't be reached any other way.

I don't know if I'll do this every year. I don't know if the war will still be grinding on next March. I hope not. But right now, the need is real, and I've got five weeks to raise the money.
